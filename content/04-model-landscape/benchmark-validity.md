> [!tldr]
> A benchmark is a measuring instrument: a set of items, a way to run the model on them and a scorer, and each part can measure something other than what the benchmark's name promises. Benchmarks break through weak tests, wrong labels, leaked test sets and saturation, and audits keep finding these flaws in popular ones. The one thing to remember: before you trust a score, ask whether a model could score high without the capability, and whether a capable model could score low.

## Key ideas

- **Construct validity comes first.** A benchmark turns a vague goal such as "reasoning" or "safety" into concrete tasks and a metric, and a high score supports the claim only as far as those tasks stand for the goal. A review of 445 LLM benchmarks with 29 expert reviewers found recurring weaknesses in how benchmarks define the phenomenon, choose tasks and score them, and it ends with eight recommendations; an earlier critique argued that a few influential benchmarks get treated as stand-ins for general progress they cannot measure ([Bean et al., “Measuring what Matters: Construct Validity in Large Language Model Benchmarks” (2025)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.04703); [Raji et al., “AI and the Everything in the Whole Wide World Benchmark” (2021)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.15366)).
- **Agentic benchmarks need task validity and outcome validity.** Task validity means a task is solvable if and only if the agent has the target capability; outcome validity means the check truly indicates success. Zhu et al. found that τ-bench let a trivial agent that returns empty responses pass 38% of its airline tasks, because doing nothing counted as success on intentionally impossible tasks, and that SWE-bench Verified's unit tests can miss important edge cases; such flaws can misjudge agent performance by up to 100% in relative terms, and their Agentic Benchmark Checklist lists the fixes ([Zhu et al., “Establishing Best Practices for Building Rigorous Agentic Benchmarks” (2025)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.02825)).
- **Weak tests and wrong labels inflate scores.** EvalPlus extended HumanEval's tests 80-fold, which lowered pass@k by up to 19.3 to 28.9% and changed the ranking of models. A manual re-annotation of 5,700 MMLU questions estimated that 6.49% of all MMLU questions contain errors, and found errors in 57% of the virology questions it analyzed ([Liu et al., “Is Your Code Generated by ChatGPT Really Correct? Rigorous Evaluation of Large Language Models for Code Generation” (2023)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.01210); [Gema et al., “Are We Done with MMLU?” (2024)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.04127)).
- **Contamination resistance is designed in.** Builders defend against it in several ways. They keep part of the test set private: SWE-Bench Pro does not publish the problems of its held-out set (12 repositories) or its commercial set (18 proprietary repositories from early-stage startups), though it releases results on the commercial set ([Deng et al., “SWE-Bench Pro: Can AI Agents Solve Long-Horizon Software Engineering Tasks?” (2025)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.16941)). They refresh items: LiveBench's authors add and update questions every month ([White et al., “LiveBench: A Challenging, Contamination-Limited LLM Benchmark” (2024)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.19314)). And they embed a canary string in every task file to help keep the tasks out of web-scraped training data, as BIG-bench does ([BIG-bench repository](https://github.com/google/BIG-bench)).
- **Benchmarks saturate, and harder ones replace them.** GLUE scores passed the non-expert human baseline about a year after its release, which led to the harder SuperGLUE. Adversarial filtering keeps new benchmarks hard for a while: WinoGrande used its AfLite algorithm to reduce dataset biases that models could exploit, and at release the best models scored 59.4 to 79.1%, depending on the amount of training data, against 94.0% for humans ([Wang et al., “SuperGLUE: A Stickier Benchmark for General-Purpose Language Understanding Systems” (2019)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.00537); [Sakaguchi et al., “WinoGrande: An Adversarial Winograd Schema Challenge at Scale” (2019)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.10641)).
- **A few items can stand in for many, within limits.** Item response theory (IRT) models the chance of answering each item correctly from the model's ability and the item's difficulty. tinyBenchmarks used it to pick 100 curated MMLU items that estimate a model's score on the full benchmark of about 14,000 items within about 2% error on average. The authors warn that the error can grow under severe distribution shift, such as a new model whose pattern of right and wrong answers differs from the models used to fit the items, and recommend refreshing the items and IRT parameters periodically ([Maia Polo et al., “tinyBenchmarks: evaluating LLMs with fewer examples” (2024)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14992)).

## Talk like an expert

Experts talk about **construct validity**, **task validity** and **outcome validity**, **label errors**, **contamination**, **held-out splits**, **adversarial filtering**, **saturation** and **IRT**, and they invoke Goodhart's law: once a score becomes the target, it stops measuring what it was built to measure. They read sample items and the grader before they read the leaderboard.
