> [!tldr]
> In a suit against Avianca, Inc., lawyers filed case law from ChatGPT, and decisions they cited "were generated by ChatGPT and do not exist". The court found bad faith based on "acts of conscious avoidance and false and misleading statements to the Court" and imposed a $5,000 penalty. Using "a reliable artificial intelligence tool" is not inherently improper, it said, but lawyers have "a gatekeeping role" over accuracy.

## Context

The plaintiff sued Avianca, asserting that "a metal serving cart struck his left knee" on a flight from El Salvador to John F. Kennedy Airport. On 13 January 2023 Avianca moved to dismiss the claims as time-barred under the Montreal Convention.

The plaintiff's firm, Levidow, Levidow & Oberman P.C., "primarily practices in New York state courts". It used a research service called Fastcase and had no Westlaw or LexisNexis accounts. The lawyer who did all the substantive work was not admitted to practice in the district. A colleague filed the notice of appearance and later signed and filed the papers.

## What happened

- **1 March 2023:** an "Affirmation in Opposition" cites and quotes decisions said to be published in the Federal Reporter, the Federal Supplement and Westlaw. The signing lawyer read it for style and "did not review any judicial authorities cited".
- **15 March 2023:** Avianca replies that its counsel "has been unable to locate most of the case law cited". No one withdraws the filing.
- **11 and 12 April 2023:** the court orders copies of the cited decisions.
- **25 April 2023:** an affidavit annexes what were purported to be copies or excerpts of all but one, which "may not be inclusive of the entire opinions but only what is made available by online database". It does not mention ChatGPT.
- **4 May 2023:** an order to show cause why the signing lawyer should not be sanctioned.
- **25 May 2023:** the drafting lawyer's affidavit gives "the first acknowledgement from any Respondent" that the filing cited bogus cases generated by ChatGPT.
- **26 May 2023:** a supplemental order to show cause covers the drafting lawyer and the firm.
- **8 June 2023:** sanctions hearing. **22 June 2023:** opinion and order.

## Impact, and how it was measured

The record is a set of findings after a hearing, not a measurement.

- **Fake decisions.** The respondents acknowledged that the “Varghese”, “Miller”, “Petersen”, “Shaboon”, “Martinez” and “Durden” decisions "were generated by ChatGPT and do not exist". Of “Varghese” the court wrote: "Its legal analysis is gibberish." The real cases the filing cited did not support its propositions, except Ashcroft v. Iqbal and, in part, Doe v. United States.
- **Harms.** The opposing party "wastes time and money in exposing the deception", and judges falsely named as authors may suffer harm to their reputation.
- **Finding.** The court found "bad faith on the part of the individual Respondents" and held the firm jointly responsible.
- **Sanctions.** "A penalty of $5,000 is jointly and severally imposed on Respondents", and within 14 days they had to send letters to the plaintiff and to each judge falsely named as an author.

**Not measured:** the opinion gives no ChatGPT version, and for the chat screenshots "no date is given for the queries". It puts no figure on the opposing side's costs, does not say how the motion to dismiss was decided, and, as a single case, says nothing about how often such errors occur.

## Why it happened

- **A gap in research tools.** When the research began, "the firm’s Fastcase account had limited access to federal cases". The drafting lawyer had not used ChatGPT before and testified that he "falsely assumed" it "was like a super search engine".
- **Prompts that asked for supporting cases.** The first asked ChatGPT to "argue that the statute of limitations is tolled by bankruptcy of defendant pursuant to montreal convention". Later ones asked for case law and "more cases", and "the chatbot complied by making them up." The filing quoted only excerpts the chatbot generated.
- **Checks that failed or were skipped.** The drafting lawyer looked up “Varghese” and "couldn’t find it", but did not reveal this in the 25 April affidavit. Asked "Is Varghese a real case", ChatGPT responded that it had supplied "real" authorities. The signing lawyer relied on the work of "a colleague of more than twenty-five years".
- **No correction after the warnings.** The individual respondents "did not begin to dribble out the truth until May 25". The court wrote that "if the matter had ended with Respondents coming clean" after the March brief or the April orders, "the record now would look quite different."

The respondents' side: the drafting lawyer testified, "I just never thought it could be made up." They cited a decision that found no bad faith in innocently relying on "the mistruths of others". The court found their case not similar: they kept advocating the fake cases after their adversary's submission told them "their citations were non-existent and could not be found".

## What changed afterwards

The firm's self-described sole equity partner acknowledged responsibility and named remedial measures, "including an expanded Fastcase subscription and CLE programming", and the firm arranged a mandatory Continuing Legal Education program "on technological competence and artificial intelligence programs". The court credited "the sincerity of Respondents when they described their embarrassment and remorse" and saw "a low likelihood that they will repeat the actions". As of the opinion, no respondent had written to withdraw the 1 March filing. The record does not say whether the measures worked.

## Lessons

The court's own lesson: "there is nothing inherently improper about using a reliable artificial intelligence tool for assistance", but "existing rules impose a gatekeeping role on attorneys to ensure the accuracy of their filings". That duty continues when "later advocating" a legal contention.

- **A chat model is not a database.** Check every citation against the primary source. See [Hallucination and factuality](#hallucination-factuality) and [Grounding and citations](#grounding-citations).
