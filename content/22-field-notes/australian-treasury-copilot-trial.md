> [!tldr]
> The Australian Department of the Treasury gave Microsoft 365 Copilot to 218 staff for 14 weeks in 2024 and evaluated the trial. Copilot could be deployed and helped with basic administrative tasks, but use fell below expectations and the evaluation found no clear evidence of better work outcomes.

> [!note] From the public record

## Context

The Australian Government ran a whole-of-government trial of Microsoft 365 Copilot from January to June 2024, coordinated by the Digital Transformation Agency (DTA). The Treasury started later because of Budget 2024–25. The report is "an internal evaluation of the Treasury Copilot trial", conducted by the Australian Centre for Evaluation (ACE).

As of the trial, Copilot ran inside Word, PowerPoint, Excel, Outlook and Teams, and was "currently the only generative AI product deployed within the Microsoft Office suite", the Treasury's dominant work platform. The evaluation team developed four use cases: generating structured content, supporting knowledge management, managing personal or process tasks, and synthesising and prioritising information.

## What happened

"The Department of the Treasury’s (Treasury) Copilot trial ran for 14 weeks, from 20 May to 23 August 2024." "A total of 218 staff participated." "The technical implementation of Copilot was smooth". The project team put support in place, including 42 self-nominated trial champions and a Copilot Trial Community chat on Microsoft Teams, but "many of these resources were underutilised".

The share of pulse survey respondents using Copilot never or less than once a week rose "from 24 per cent in pulse survey 2, to 29 per cent, 38 per cent, and 40 per cent in the subsequent pulse surveys". The report notes their low response rate but reads them as "an indication of decreasing rates of usage of Copilot for non-super users". Participants "retained their licences following the formal trial period (unless they indicated otherwise)".

## Impact, and how it was measured

ACE used a pre-trial survey (n=153), a post-trial survey (n=136), five pulse surveys, a manager survey (n=49), focus groups and case studies. "Note that these analyses were descriptive in nature". Pre- and post-trial answers were matched for 100 respondents.

- **Use.** "Almost one-quarter of post-trial survey respondents (22 per cent) reported using Copilot 4–5 times per week or more".
- **Speed.** "49 per cent of respondents agreed or strongly agreed that Copilot improved the speed at which they completed tasks". Before the trial, "85 per cent reported expecting that Copilot would improve the speed at which they complete tasks".
- **Workload.** "59 per cent of post-trial survey respondents reported that Copilot supported them to perform little to none" (0 to 25 per cent) of their weekly workload more effectively.
- **Quality.** "39 per cent of post-trial survey respondents agreed or strongly agreed that Copilot had improved the quality of their work", and "65 per cent of managers reported that Copilot had no average impact on team members’ work quality". The summary finding: "the evaluation did not find clear evidence that Copilot helped improve work outcomes during the short trial period".
- **Sentiment.** Before the trial, no participant reported negative sentiment. Afterwards, "one in 5 (21 per cent) post-trial survey respondents reported negative sentiment toward using Copilot at Treasury".
- **Cost.** ACE estimates that a staff member on the current APS6 salary "would need to redirect approximately 13 minutes of time per week to higher value tasks" to offset the current licence cost. The trial data "did not quantify time savings", but the report says the results suggest that productivity benefits and time savings "are likely to offset the licence costs".

The counts differ between sections: against the 218 participants and n=136 above, response rates are calculated "from a total of 232 trial participants", and Appendix B reports that "164 respondents completed the survey" after the trial.

**Not measured:** time saved, since "the evaluation did not explicitly measure time saved for basic administrative tasks". The report describes no testing of outputs for accuracy. The evaluation had no comparison group ("The lack of a robust ‘counterfactual’"), and relied mainly on voluntary self-reports from staff who applied to take part.

## Why it happened

The report links the lower-than-expected use to several factors:

- **Expectations.** Recruitment materials indicated that the product "is powered by the same technology as ChatGPT, and may deliver productivity benefits". "Unrealistically high expectations at the trial outset may have contributed to the problem", as some staff were discouraged by the product's performance and gave up using it.
- **Security restrictions.** Copilot did not perform as well as generative AI products that staff had used elsewhere. "In part, this was due to restrictions imposed by the Treasury’s IT security environment."
- **Accuracy and checking.** Participants reported incorrect summaries, and "These limitations sometimes led to the generation of fictional content". Coaching Copilot and checking its outputs "was sometimes more time intensive than creating the output themselves". See [Hallucination and factuality](#hallucination-factuality).
- **Training and time.** Staff asked for more tailored training and found learning time hard to fit into their workload. A Microsoft representative suggested to the project team that "it takes up to 12 weeks for an individual to learn how to use the product effectively", similar to the length of the trial.

## What changed afterwards

The report makes seven product-agnostic recommendations, contingent on whether the Treasury rolls out generative AI: clear use cases and managed expectations; a phased rollout; an assessment of investment in training, since Teams forums and champions "did not provide the technical support participants needed"; guidelines on disclosure; longer-term monitoring; staff outcomes as secondary outcomes; and periodic checks for products that fit the Treasury's security requirements. It does not state what the Treasury decided.

## Lessons

The source's own conclusion: "The results of this trial demonstrate that generative AI can be deployed within Treasury, but there are some limitations." It finds that "the current iteration of Copilot is not sophisticated enough to support complex work tasks" without substantial input, and reports an unanticipated benefit: its ability to "contribute to accessibility and inclusion for neurodivergent and part-time staff".

The wiki's reading:

- **Expectations are part of the deployment.** Narrow, specific use cases set a bar a tool can meet. See [Scoping AI features](#ai-use-case-scoping).
- **Volunteers' self-reports cannot attribute impact.** A comparison group is what turns a trial into evidence. See [Online controlled experiments for AI features](#online-experiments) and [Measuring the impact and ROI of an AI feature](#measuring-ai-impact).
- **A break-even estimate is not a measurement.** The 13 minutes say how much time Copilot would need to free up, not how much it freed.
