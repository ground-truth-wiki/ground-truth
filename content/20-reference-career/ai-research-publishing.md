> [!tldr]
> Much AI research appears first as an arXiv preprint, which is moderated but not peer reviewed, and only later, if ever, passes review at a conference such as NeurIPS, ICML or ICLR. Conference review can be partly public, but it is noisy and overloaded, and labs also publish technical reports, model cards and blog posts that nobody referees. The one thing to remember: a paper's status (preprint, accepted, reproduced) tells you how much weight it can bear, so check it before you cite it or build on it.

## Key ideas

- **Preprints often come first, and arXiv does not peer review them.** arXiv, founded by Paul Ginsparg in 1991 and an independent nonprofit since 2026, only moderates submissions for topic and scholarly value; its endorsement step for new authors "is not peer review", AI papers are listed in categories such as cs.LG (machine learning), cs.CL (computation and language), cs.AI (artificial intelligence) and cs.CV (computer vision and pattern recognition), and more than one or two cross-lists to other categories is "rarely appropriate" ([About arXiv](https://info.arxiv.org/about/index.html); [arXiv, “Endorsement”](https://info.arxiv.org/help/endorsement.html); [arXiv category taxonomy](https://arxiv.org/category_taxonomy); [arXiv, “Category cross listing”](https://info.arxiv.org/help/cross.html)). Since 31 October 2025, arXiv's computer science section has required review articles and position papers to be accepted at a journal or conference, after peer review, before they are submitted, because generative AI made such papers "fast and easy to write" and "the majority of the review articles" it received were "little more than annotated bibliographies" ([arXiv blog, 31 October 2025](https://blog.arxiv.org/2025/10/31/attention-authors-updated-practice-for-review-articles-and-position-papers-in-arxiv-cs-category/)).
- **Conferences are where peer review happens.** NeurIPS (held since 1987), ICML and ICLR are the three primary high-impact machine learning conferences, and ICLR has used open peer review since its first meeting in 2013: its official reviews are anonymous but publicly visible on OpenReview and stay online even for rejected papers ([NeurIPS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conference_on_Neural_Information_Processing_Systems); [ICLR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Conference_on_Learning_Representations); [ICLR 2026 Author Guide](https://iclr.cc/Conferences/2026/AuthorGuide)). NLP venues such as ACL, EMNLP, NAACL and EACL take papers reviewed through ACL Rolling Review, where authors get reviews in a cycle, may revise, and then commit the paper to a venue that decides; in vision, the nonprofit Computer Vision Foundation sponsors CVPR, ICCV and WACV; and OpenReview itself is a nonprofit platform from UMass Amherst that lets each venue choose how open its reviewing is ([ACL Rolling Review](https://aclrollingreview.org/); [ARR dates and venues](https://aclrollingreview.org/dates); [Computer Vision Foundation](https://www.thecvf.com/); [About OpenReview](https://openreview.net/about)).
- **Review is noisy, and the load keeps growing.** In the NeurIPS 2021 consistency experiment two independent committees disagreed on 23% of the papers they both reviewed, and about half of the accepted papers would change if the process were rerun; a follow-up on the 2014 experiment found that reviewer scores of accepted papers did not predict their citations seven years later ([Beygelzimer et al., “Has the Machine Learning Review Process Become More Arbitrary as the Field Has Grown? The NeurIPS 2021 Consistency Experiment” (2023)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03262); [Cortes and Lawrence, “Inconsistency in Conference Peer Review: Revisiting the 2014 NeurIPS Experiment” (2021)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.09774)). NeurIPS submissions grew from 9,467 in 2020 to 21,575 valid main-track submissions in 2025, when 5,290 were accepted and 20,518 reviewers took part; ICLR 2026 received 19,525 valid submissions, desk-rejected 779 (among them every paper with confirmed hallucinated references), ran two LLM content detectors over every review, and reset review scores to their pre-rebuttal state after a malicious user exploited the OpenReview API, in the middle of the discussion period, to release the identities of authors, reviewers and area chairs for a large subset of submissions ([NeurIPS 2025 program chairs](https://blog.neurips.cc/2025/09/30/reflections-on-the-2025-review-process-from-the-program-committee-chairs/); [ICLR 2026 retrospective](https://blog.iclr.cc/2026/03/31/a-retrospective-on-the-iclr-2026-review-process/)).
- **Reports, cards and blogs are a parallel channel that nobody referees.** Labs also release technical reports, model and system cards, blog posts and code, none of which goes through peer review before release; model cards were proposed to document a model's intended use and its evaluation across groups and conditions ([Mitchell et al., “Model Cards for Model Reporting” (2018)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.03993)), as covered in [Reading model cards and system cards](#model-documentation). Read them as the developer's own measurements, not as replicable science: the GPT-4 technical report says it "contains no further details about the architecture (including model size), hardware, training compute, dataset construction, training method, or similar" ([OpenAI, “GPT-4 Technical Report” (2023)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.08774)).
- **Reproducibility is a known weak spot.** NeurIPS 2019 added a code-submission policy, a community reproducibility challenge and a reproducibility checklist ([Pineau et al., “Improving Reproducibility in Machine Learning Research (A Report from the NeurIPS 2019 Reproducibility Program)” (2020)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.12206)), and as of November 2026 papers without the NeurIPS checklist are desk-rejected, while an honest "no" with a justification is accepted and code release is not required ([NeurIPS Paper Checklist Guidelines](https://neurips.cc/public/guides/PaperChecklist)). Beyond core ML, a survey found 20 papers from 17 fields that use ML which together report errors affecting 329 papers, with data leakage a pitfall in every case, and in a reproduction of civil-war prediction studies, complex models did not perform substantively better than logistic regression once leakage was fixed (see [Validation, tuning and leakage](#validation-tuning)) ([Kapoor and Narayanan, “Leakage and the Reproducibility Crisis in ML-based Science” (2022)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.07048)).
- **Judge the claim, then cite the exact version.** Choosing different benchmark tasks can significantly change how methods perform relative to each other, a "benchmark lottery", and gains credited to a new architecture sometimes come from hyperparameter tuning instead, so look for the venue and its public review history, released code, tuned baselines and ablations that isolate the claimed cause before trusting a state-of-the-art claim (see [Benchmark literacy](#benchmark-literacy)) ([Dehghani et al., “The Benchmark Lottery” (2021)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.07002); [Lipton and Steinhardt, “Troubling Trends in Machine Learning Scholarship” (2018)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.03341)). When you cite a preprint, include its arXiv identifier with the version number so readers see "the specific version intended", look at the arXiv record's journal reference and DOI fields, where authors can add the peer-reviewed publication, and check every citation against the original, as [The Canon](#the-canon) advises ([arXiv, “References to and in arXiv Documents”](https://info.arxiv.org/help/faq/references.html)).

## Talk like an expert

Researchers say a result is "on arXiv" (not yet reviewed) or "at NeurIPS" (accepted), talk about rebuttals, desk rejects, camera-ready versions, ablations and SOTA-chasing, and ask "what was the baseline, and was it tuned as hard as the new method?" before they look at the headline number.

## Go deeper

- [Beygelzimer et al., “Has the Machine Learning Review Process Become More Arbitrary as the Field Has Grown? The NeurIPS 2021 Consistency Experiment” (2023)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03262): how often two committees disagree, and why more selectivity means more arbitrariness.
- [Pineau et al., “Improving Reproducibility in Machine Learning Research (A Report from the NeurIPS 2019 Reproducibility Program)” (2020)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.12206): the NeurIPS 2019 code policy, reproducibility challenge and checklist.
- [Kapoor and Narayanan, “Leakage and the Reproducibility Crisis in ML-based Science” (2022)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.07048): a taxonomy of eight types of leakage and the model info sheets that expose them.
- [NeurIPS Paper Checklist Guidelines](https://neurips.cc/public/guides/PaperChecklist): the sixteen questions every NeurIPS paper must answer, from claims and limitations to compute and LLM use.
