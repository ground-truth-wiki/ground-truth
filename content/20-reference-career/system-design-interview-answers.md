> [!tldr]
> Five prompts cover common shapes of a generative AI system design interview: an enterprise knowledge assistant, a personal assistant, a coding feature, a support agent that acts, and a writing feature such as smart compose. A strong answer names the closest blueprint in one sentence, then spends the time on what interviewers grade: clarifying questions, an eval plan, an estimate, the defining risk and the trade-offs.

## Why it matters
Drawing a retrieval pipeline is the easy part. Saying what you would measure, what a request costs, and which failure would stop the launch is harder. Worked answers give you a target to practice against, and the weak answers to avoid. This chapter applies the [system design method](#system-design-method) and the [reference architectures](#reference-architectures) under a clock, as part of [interview prep](#interview-prep).

## Intuition
The blueprints are the answer key everyone has already read. An interview is more like a driving test: the route is known, and the examiner watches how you check the mirrors. In a design interview, the mirrors are the questions you ask, the numbers you put on the board, and the risk you name before anyone asks.

## How it works
**What changes from the interviews you know.** Three kinds of design interview share a whiteboard but center on different things:

| Interview | Centers on | What you estimate |
|---|---|---|
| Distributed systems | Storage, partitioning, caching, consistency | Requests per second, bytes stored |
| Classic ML system design | Project setup, data pipeline, modeling, serving | Training data, inference latency |
| Generative AI system design | Defining quality, context and retrieval, tool boundaries, safety | Tokens and model calls per request, cost per user |

The first row draws on the chapters of ByteByteGo’s [System Design Interview](https://bytebytego.com/courses/system-design-interview); the second is the process in Chip Huyen’s [Machine Learning Systems Design](https://huyenchip.com/machine-learning-systems-design/toc.html). In the third you rarely train the model, so modeling shrinks to model choice and prompting, while evaluation grows: open-ended text has no single right label. Gateways, rate limits and caches still sit underneath, in the shared [reference architecture](#reference-architecture). Every answer below follows Anthropic’s advice in [Building effective agents](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents): start simple and add complexity only when needed.

**Five worked answers.** Each is a blueprint plus the interview layer, on the 45-minute budget. In a real interview, state your own assumptions.

What interviewers probe in each, and the weak answer they hear:

- **Knowledge assistant: permissions and freshness.** Filter inside the retrieval query and test leaks; [OWASP LLM08:2025](https://genai.owasp.org/llmrisk/llm082025-vector-and-embedding-weaknesses/) recommends permission-aware vector stores. Microsoft’s [Copilot privacy page](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/copilot/plan/microsoft-365-copilot-privacy) says it “only surfaces organizational data to which individual users have at least view permissions” and asks customers to make sure the right people have access to the right content (as of November 2026): the assistant inherits whatever permissions already exist. Weak: “we embed everything and filter the results.”
- **Personal assistant: memory, cost and injection.** Any email or page it reads can carry instructions ([Greshake et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.12173)); private data, untrusted content and a way to send data out form the [lethal trifecta](https://simonwillison.net/2025/Jun/16/the-lethal-trifecta/). Break the trifecta, confirm sends and payments, and show memory to the user. Weak: “a guardrail model blocks injections.”
- **Coding: which problem?** Completion is about latency and acceptance: for GitHub Copilot, the acceptance rate of shown suggestions tracked perceived productivity better than whether completions stayed in the code ([Ziegler et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.06537)). An agent is about verification and isolation: score patches against real tests, as SWE-bench does ([Jimenez et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.06770)), in a [sandbox](#sandboxes) without production credentials. Weak: “the model writes the code and we merge it.”
- **Support agent: limits, consistency, liability.** Limits live in tool code, not the prompt, and the company answers for what its agent says ([support agents](#support-agents)). [τ-bench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.12045) (Yao et al., 2024) introduced pass^k and found 2024’s state-of-the-art function-calling agents below 25% at pass^8 in its retail domain. Weak: “we track deflection.”
- **Smart compose: no blueprint, close to classic ML.** Gmail’s [Smart Compose](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.00080) (Chen et al., 2019) needed 90th-percentile latency under 60 ms with inference on almost every keystroke, chose an LSTM language model over a higher-quality Transformer because of that limit and the request volume, showed suggestions only above a confidence threshold, and dropped suggestions with gender pronouns after finding gender-occupation bias. Weak: “call the largest model on every keystroke.”

**Practice prompts, without answers.** Give each 45 minutes:

1. A meeting summarizer for a sales team that writes notes back to the CRM.
2. An agent that matches supplier invoices to purchase orders and flags exceptions.
3. Phone ordering for a restaurant chain, by voice.
4. Real-time translation inside a chat app.
5. Natural-language search over a company’s code and design documents.
6. Moderation for images that users upload to a marketplace.

> [!try]
> Take prompt 2 with a five-minute timer and write only the clarifying questions and success criteria. Fewer than five questions, or no way to measure success? Start again.

## Numbers to know
> [!number]
> Rules of thumb. About a third of the time each for requirements and quality, architecture and data, and operations, risks and trade-offs ([system design method](#system-design-method)). 1 million requests a day is about 12 per second. A first eval set: 100 to 200 real cases. At 90% success per independent run, all 8 of 8 runs succeed only about 43% of the time (0.9⁸ ≈ 0.43). Sourced: Smart Compose required 90th-percentile latency under 60 ms ([Chen et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.00080)).

## Pitfalls and anti-patterns
> [!pitfall] Reciting the blueprint
> Ten minutes of ingest, embed, retrieve and generate tells the interviewer nothing new. Name the blueprint in one sentence; spend the time on its defining component and main risk.

> [!pitfall] Answering a question nobody asked
> “Design a coding assistant” can mean inline completion (a latency problem) or an agent (an isolation and review problem). Ask which before you draw anything.

- **An estimate with no decision.** Every number should change the model tier, caching or hosting.
- **“We will use an LLM judge.”** Uncalibrated against human labels, it is one more unverified model ([LLM as a judge](#llm-as-judge)).
- **Safety as a box.** A filter is not a boundary; say what the model cannot reach.

## Talk like an expert
The vocabulary: **blueprint**, **defining component**, **eval plan**, **cost per request**, **permission-aware retrieval**, **lethal trifecta**, **pass^k**, **acceptance rate**. Experts restate the prompt as a measurable goal, list assumptions, and say what evidence would change the design. Live debates in hiring: how much classic ML depth a generative AI design interview should test, and how to assess candidates who use AI tools every day.

> [!hottake] The candidate who names no model gives a stronger answer than the one who names three.
> **The counterargument:** model choice sets cost, latency, context limits and where data may go, and naming none can sound like no hands-on experience. The best answers name a class of model and the eval that would choose between candidates.
