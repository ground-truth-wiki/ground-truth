> [!tldr]
> Dangerous-capability evaluations test whether a model could meaningfully help someone cause severe harm, such as a biological weapon or a cyberattack, or could act dangerously on its own, such as copying itself or automating AI research. Their results decide which safeguards a model ships with under its developer's [frontier safety framework](#frontier-safety-frameworks), and as of November 2026 laws in the EU and California ask for them, with New York following in January 2027. Remember that a pass only means this test, at this level of effort, found nothing: weak elicitation and models that recognize tests can both hide a capability.

## Key ideas

- **Every domain asks an uplift question.** Frontier frameworks set capability thresholds for severe harms, such as substantially assisting with CBRN weapons development, and require mitigations once a model crosses one ([Frontier Model Forum, “Third-Party Assessments”](https://www.frontiermodelforum.org/uploads/2025/08/FMF-Technical-Report-on-Third-Party-Assessments-2.pdf) (August 2025)). Evaluations also cover persuasion and deception, cybersecurity, self-proliferation and self-reasoning (Phuong et al., [“Evaluating Frontier Models for Dangerous Capabilities”](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.13793) (2024)), and as of November 2026 Google DeepMind tracks harmful manipulation and machine-learning R&D ([Google DeepMind, “Strengthening our Frontier Safety Framework”](https://deepmind.google/discover/blog/strengthening-our-frontier-safety-framework/)) while Anthropic's RSP v3 adds high-stakes sabotage, such as a model manipulating how its successors are trained (Williams and Freund, [“Anthropic's RSP v3.0: how it works, what's changed, and some reflections”](https://www.governance.ai/analysis/anthropics-rsp-v3-0-how-it-works-whats-changed-and-some-reflections) (GovAI, 2026)); see [measuring time horizons](#measuring-time-horizons) for AI R&D.
- **Proxies are cheap; agentic tasks are closer to the threat.** WMDP's 3,668 multiple-choice questions stand in for hazardous knowledge in biosecurity, cybersecurity and chemical security, and also benchmark unlearning: the authors' RMU method lowered WMDP scores while keeping general biology and computer science ability (Li et al., [“The WMDP Benchmark”](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.03218) (2024)), but a knowledge test may capture general science rather than dangerous capability, which is one thing third-party reviewers check ([Frontier Model Forum, 2025](https://www.frontiermodelforum.org/uploads/2025/08/FMF-Technical-Report-on-Third-Party-Assessments-2.pdf)). Cybench gives agents 40 professional capture-the-flag tasks with subtasks and the first human solve time of each; in 2024, agents without subtask guidance solved complete tasks that took human teams up to 11 minutes, against 24 hours 54 minutes for the hardest task (Zhang et al., [“Cybench”](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.08926) (ICLR 2025)), while CyberSecEval tests insecure code suggestions and help with cyberattacks (Bhatt et al., [“Purple Llama CyberSecEval”](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.04724) (2023)).
- **Uplift trials and expert assessments measure the threat more directly, at high cost.** Anthropic's bioweapons acquisition uplift trial gave groups of 8 to 10 participants up to two days to draft an acquisition plan, with a control group limited to basic internet resources and a treatment group that also had Claude with safeguards removed, graded by Deloitte against a rubric; in the Future of Life Institute's AI Safety Index of July 2025 it was the only human uplift trial reported among seven developers ([FLI, “Human uplift trials” indicator](https://futureoflife.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/Indicator-Human_uplift_trials.pdf) (2025)). System cards pair benchmarks with expert work: OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra card lists ExploitBench, expert-led assessments and external cyber evaluations by Irregular ([OpenAI, “GPT-6 Astra System Card”](https://deploymentsafety.openai.com/gpt-6-astra) (September 2026)).
- **Elicitation decides what a score means.** METR's guidelines ask evaluators to use the highest-performing fine-tune, model version or scaffold, fix spurious failures, and declare that they know no easy way to improve the score dramatically, and the authors call their own recommendations “somewhat informed and reasoned guesses only” ([METR, “Guidelines for capability elicitation”](https://metr.org/blog/2024-03-15-guidelines-for-capability-elicitation/) (2024)). Models can be prompted or password-locked to underperform on dangerous-capability tests while keeping their general scores (van der Weij et al., [“AI Sandbagging”](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.07358) (2024)), and because models increasingly tell test settings from real deployment, dangerous capabilities could go undetected before deployment ([International AI Safety Report 2026](https://internationalaisafetyreport.org/publication/international-ai-safety-report-2026)); see [scheming, sandbagging and evaluation awareness](#scheming-evaluations).
- **Results map to thresholds through judgment, often as “cannot rule out”.** Anthropic activated ASL-3 protections for Claude Opus 4 on 22 May 2025 as a precaution, because “clearly ruling out ASL-3 risks is not possible”, without having determined that the model crossed the threshold ([Anthropic, “Activating AI Safety Level 3 protections”](https://www.anthropic.com/news/activating-asl3-protections)), and OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra system card, published 3 September 2026, says “Astra is our first model to reach the Critical level of cybersecurity capability under our Preparedness Framework” ([OpenAI, 2026](https://deploymentsafety.openai.com/gpt-6-astra)). As of November 2026, laws lean on the same work: the EU AI Act requires providers of general-purpose models with systemic risk to evaluate them with state-of-the-art protocols, including adversarial testing ([AI Act, Article 55](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/55/)), and California's SB 53 and New York's amended RAISE Act (which applies from 1 January 2027) require the transparency reports of large frontier developers to summarize catastrophic-risk assessments and the involvement of third-party evaluators ([Cooley, April 2026](https://www.cooley.com/news/insight/2026/2026-03-31-new-yorks-frontier-ai-law-gets-a-california-makeover-with-some-key-differences)).
- **Developers are not the only evaluators, and access shapes independence.** Government bodies such as the UK [AI Security Institute](https://www.aisi.gov.uk/) and the US center at NIST, which the July 2025 Action Plan tasked with evaluating frontier systems for CBRNE and cyber risks ([America's AI Action Plan](https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/Americas-AI-Action-Plan.pdf)) and which NIST's page names, as of November 2026, the Center for Advancing Innovation and Standards for Super Intelligence ([NIST](https://www.nist.gov/caisi)), test models alongside independent organizations such as [METR](https://metr.org/) and [Apollo Research](https://www.apolloresearch.ai/), and the open [Inspect Evals](https://ukgovernmentbeis.github.io/inspect_evals/) registry offers shared implementations of WMDP, Cybench and CyberSecEval. External evaluators often get limited model access, little information and short timeframes, and Charnock et al. propose three access levels, from black-box with minimal information to white-box with comprehensive information (Charnock et al., [“Expanding External Access To Frontier AI Models For Dangerous Capability Evaluations”](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.11916) (2026)); the Frontier Model Forum frames third-party assessments as complementing, not replacing, internal safety processes ([Frontier Model Forum, 2025](https://www.frontiermodelforum.org/uploads/2025/08/FMF-Technical-Report-on-Third-Party-Assessments-2.pdf)).

## Planned visuals

- `diagram`: from threat model to release decision: risk domain, threat model, evaluation formats (knowledge proxy, agentic task suite, human uplift trial, expert assessment), elicitation, threshold judgment and safeguards, with institutes and third-party evaluators attached to the steps they review.
- `chart`: an illustrative elicitation curve, measured score against elicitation effort (plain prompt, agent scaffold, tools, fine-tuning, many attempts), showing how the same model can sit below a threshold at low effort and above it at high effort.
- `compare`: a knowledge proxy benchmark next to a human uplift trial: cost, realism, sample size, what each measures and what each misses.

## Talk like an expert

Experts speak of **uplift**, **threat models**, **proxy tasks**, **elicitation**, **capability thresholds**, **sandbagging** and **evaluation awareness**, and they read the dangerous-capability section of a system card or institute report by asking what was elicited, by whom, with what access, and whether the conclusion is "below the threshold" or only "cannot rule out" (see [reading model cards and system cards](#model-documentation)).
