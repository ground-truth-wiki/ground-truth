> [!tldr]
> Since the 1950s, "AI" has meant logic and search, then hand-written expert rules, then statistics, and now large neural networks trained on vast data. The first two waves brought real progress but promised more than they delivered, and the downturns that followed are remembered as "AI winters" of cut funding, though one historian argues that the first was never a broad collapse. The one thing to remember: general methods that scale with computation, such as learning from data, have tended to win in the long run (Rich Sutton's "bitter lesson"), and today's frontier is one more dated snapshot in that story.

## Key ideas

- **"AI" is a moving target.** Russell and Norvig's textbook sorts definitions by whether a system should think or act, and whether like a human or rationally, and it adopts the rational-agent view; the EU AI Act, as adopted in 2024, instead defines an "AI system" in Article 3(1) as a machine-based system that is designed to operate with varying levels of autonomy and infers from its input how to generate outputs such as predictions, content, recommendations or decisions (see [Governance, regulation and law](#governance-regulation)). Meanwhile, whatever starts to work tends to stop being called AI, a pattern known as the AI effect ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Artificial Intelligence”](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/artificial-intelligence/); [Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Article 3](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj); [AI effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AI_effect)).
- **It started as logic and search.** Turing's 1950 paper swapped the question "Can machines think?" for an imitation game, and the 1955 proposal for a summer workshop at Dartmouth in 1956 named the field, on the conjecture that every feature of intelligence can be described so precisely that a machine can simulate it ([Turing, “Computing Machinery and Intelligence” (1950)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/LIX.236.433); [McCarthy et al., “A Proposal for the Dartmouth Summer Research Project on Artificial Intelligence” (1955)](https://www-formal.stanford.edu/jmc/history/dartmouth/dartmouth.html)). Symbolic programs such as the Logic Theorist (proving theorems), ELIZA (scripted conversation) and SHRDLU (English commands in a world of toy blocks) fed predictions that AI would be solved "within a generation" (Minsky, 1967), while perception and movement, easy for a one-year-old, proved far harder than logic, a pattern later named Moravec's paradox ([History of artificial intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_artificial_intelligence); [Moravec's paradox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moravec%27s_paradox)).
- **Optimism outran results, twice.** After the ALPAC report (1966) dried up US machine-translation funding, the UK's Lighthill report (1973) blamed the "combinatorial explosion" and led the British government to end support for AI research in most universities, and US defense funding moved to mission-oriented work; historian Thomas Haigh argues that this "first AI winter" hit a few elite labs while the field kept growing, since ACM's AI interest group SIGART went from 1,241 members in 1973 to 3,500 by mid-1978 ([Lighthill, “Artificial Intelligence: A General Survey” (1973)](http://www.chilton-computing.org.uk/inf/literature/reports/lighthill_report/p001.htm); [Lighthill report](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lighthill_report); [Haigh, “There Was No ‘First AI Winter’” (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1145/3625833)). Building on earlier systems such as DENDRAL (chemistry) and MYCIN (infectious disease), the 1980s brought a boom in commercial expert systems such as XCON (configuring DEC's VAX computers). That boom, together with Japan's Fifth Generation project, ended in a second winter after the market for specialized Lisp machines collapsed in 1987 ([AI winter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AI_winter); [Expert system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expert_system); [XCON](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xcon); [History of artificial intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_artificial_intelligence)).
- **Learning from data took over.** Backpropagation, popularized in 1986, revived neural networks ("connectionism") as a rival to symbol manipulation, and through the 1990s and 2000s the mainstream turned statistical: probabilistic reasoning, reinforcement learning, kernel methods such as support-vector machines, and statistical machine translation ([Rumelhart et al., “Learning representations by back-propagating errors” (1986)](https://doi.org/10.1038/323533a0); [Cortes and Vapnik, “Support-vector networks” (1995)](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00994018); [Brown et al., “A Statistical Approach to Machine Translation” (1990)](https://aclanthology.org/J90-2002/); [History of artificial intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_artificial_intelligence)). Deep learning took off when large labeled datasets met GPUs: ImageNet supplied millions of labeled images, and AlexNet, a convolutional network trained on GPUs, won the 2012 ImageNet challenge with far fewer errors than the runner-up ([Deng et al., “ImageNet: A Large-Scale Hierarchical Image Database” (2009)](https://doi.org/10.1109/CVPR.2009.5206848); [Krizhevsky et al., “ImageNet Classification with Deep Convolutional Neural Networks” (2012)](https://papers.nips.cc/paper/2012/hash/c399862d3b9d6b76c8436e924a68c45b-Abstract.html); [History of artificial intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_artificial_intelligence)).
- **Deep learning spread from vision to everything.** Over the next decade the same toolkit gave word vectors (2013), neural translation with sequence-to-sequence models and attention (2014), very deep residual networks (2015), AlphaGo, which paired deep networks with tree search to beat the European Go champion 5 to 0 (reported in 2016), and AlphaFold, far more accurate than competing methods at predicting protein structures (2021) ([Mikolov et al., “Efficient Estimation of Word Representations in Vector Space” (2013)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1301.3781); [Sutskever et al., “Sequence to Sequence Learning with Neural Networks” (2014)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1409.3215); [Bahdanau et al., “Neural Machine Translation by Jointly Learning to Align and Translate” (2014)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1409.0473); [He et al., “Deep Residual Learning for Image Recognition” (2015)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1512.03385); [Silver et al., “Mastering the game of Go with deep neural networks and tree search” (2016)](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature16961); [Jumper et al., “Highly accurate protein structure prediction with AlphaFold” (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-021-03819-2)). The shift was recognized by the 2018 Turing Award to Yoshua Bengio, Geoffrey Hinton and Yann LeCun, and by the 2024 Nobel Prizes in Physics (John Hopfield and Hinton, for neural networks) and Chemistry (David Baker for computational protein design, and Demis Hassabis and John Jumper for protein structure prediction) ([Turing Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turing_Award); [Science News on the physics prize](https://www.sciencenews.org/article/nobel-physics-2024-machine-learning); [Science News on the chemistry prize](https://www.sciencenews.org/article/nobel-prize-chemistry-2024-proteins)).
- **Transformers turned language models into assistants.** The transformer (2017), easier to parallelize than recurrent networks, became the basis for pretraining ever larger models, from BERT (2018) and GPT-2 (2019) to GPT-3 with 175 billion parameters (2020), and their loss fell along smooth power laws in model size, data and compute ([Vaswani et al., “Attention Is All You Need” (2017)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03762); [Devlin et al., “BERT: Pre-training of Deep Bidirectional Transformers for Language Understanding” (2018)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.04805); [Radford et al., “Language Models are Unsupervised Multitask Learners” (2019)](https://cdn.openai.com/better-language-models/language_models_are_unsupervised_multitask_learners.pdf); [Brown et al., “Language Models are Few-Shot Learners” (2020)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.14165); [Kaplan et al., “Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models” (2020)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.08361)). Fine-tuning with human feedback made models follow instructions, so that on the authors' test prompts human raters preferred the outputs of a 1.3-billion-parameter InstructGPT over those of the 175-billion-parameter GPT-3, and ChatGPT, launched on 30 November 2022, was reported to have reached 100 million monthly active users two months later; what came next, reasoning models and agents, is tracked in the [state-of-the-art snapshot](#sota-snapshot) (as of November 2026) ([Ouyang et al., “Training language models to follow instructions with human feedback” (2022)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.02155); [ChatGPT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ChatGPT)).

## Talk like an expert

Experts place a new system in a lineage (symbolic, statistical, connectionist) and reach for a few recurring patterns: hype followed by winter, the AI effect, Moravec's paradox, and the bitter lesson, from [Sutton, “The Bitter Lesson” (2019)](http://www.incompleteideas.net/IncIdeas/BitterLesson.html), that "general methods that leverage computation are ultimately the most effective". The standing counterpoint is the neuro-symbolic comeback, in systems such as AlphaGeometry ([Trinh et al., “Solving olympiad geometry without human demonstrations” (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-023-06747-5)), which pairs a neural language model with a symbolic deduction engine.

## Go deeper

- [Russell and Norvig, *Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach*, 4th edition](https://aima.cs.berkeley.edu/): the standard textbook; chapter 1 introduces the field.
- [Turing, “Computing Machinery and Intelligence” (1950)](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/LIX.236.433): the paper that proposed the imitation game.
- [Haigh, “There Was No ‘First AI Winter’” (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1145/3625833): a historian's challenge to the standard boom-and-bust story, from his Historical Reflections column in Communications of the ACM, part of a series on the history of AI.
- [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Artificial Intelligence”](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/artificial-intelligence/): definitions, the Dartmouth workshop and the main approaches.
